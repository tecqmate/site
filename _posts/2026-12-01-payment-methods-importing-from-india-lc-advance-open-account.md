Choosing how to pay is one of the biggest decisions in an import deal. The supplier wants to be sure they will be paid. You want to be sure that the goods you ordered will arrive as agreed. Every payment method shifts risk between the two of you.

This guide explains the common options in plain language, with the risks of each and when it makes sense.

## Quick answer

For a **first order with a new supplier**, avoid paying everything in advance. A **split payment** (a deposit before production and the balance against shipping documents or after inspection) is a common middle ground. For larger orders, a **letter of credit** gives strong protection to both sides, at a higher cost and with more paperwork. **Open account** terms are for established relationships, after trust has been built.

Always send money only to a bank account in the **verified legal name** of the supplier.

## The main payment methods

### 1. Advance payment (prepayment)

You pay all or part of the price before the supplier ships.

- **Risk for you:** high. If the supplier does not deliver, or sends the wrong goods, you must recover the money.
- **Risk for the supplier:** low.
- **Cost:** usually just bank transfer fees.
- **Best for:** small orders, samples, or suppliers you have verified and worked with before.

Full advance payment to a new, unverified supplier is the most common route to losing money.

### 2. Split payment (deposit plus balance)

You pay a deposit when the order is confirmed, and the rest at an agreed point, such as before shipment, against copies of shipping documents, or after inspection.

- **Risk for you:** medium. The deposit is at risk, but the balance is held back until something is proven.
- **Risk for the supplier:** medium. They have a commitment from you and part payment.
- **Best for:** first and second orders with a supplier you have verified.

The point where the balance is paid matters. "Balance after inspection and before shipping" gives you more protection than "balance before the goods leave the factory".

### 3. Letter of credit (LC)

You ask your bank to issue a letter of credit. It is a promise from the bank to pay the supplier once the supplier presents documents that match the LC terms exactly.

- **Risk for you:** lower. Payment is only released against compliant documents, such as the invoice, packing list and bill of lading.
- **Risk for the supplier:** lower, because a bank has promised to pay.
- **Cost:** bank fees and more paperwork. A small error in a document can delay payment.
- **Best for:** larger orders, new relationships, and when both sides want a bank in the middle.

An LC protects you against non-shipment, but it checks **documents**, not the **goods**. If the documents look correct but the product quality is poor, the bank will not know. Combine an LC with a pre-shipment inspection.

International rules for LCs are published by the International Chamber of Commerce (ICC) and are widely used. Ask your bank which version and terms apply.

### 4. Documentary collection (D/P and D/A)

The supplier ships the goods and sends the shipping documents through their bank to your bank. Your bank releases the documents when you:

- **D/P (documents against payment):** pay, or
- **D/A (documents against acceptance):** accept a promise to pay on a later date.

- **Risk for you:** lower than advance payment, since you get the documents only when you pay or accept.
- **Risk for the supplier:** higher than with an LC, because the bank does not guarantee payment. If you refuse the documents, the supplier has goods abroad and no payment.
- **Best for:** established relationships, where trust is moderate, and lower cost matters.

### 5. Open account

The supplier ships the goods and you pay later, for example 30, 60 or 90 days after shipment or delivery.

- **Risk for you:** low.
- **Risk for the supplier:** high. They depend on your promise to pay.
- **Best for:** long-term relationships. Suppliers often offer it only to buyers with a good record, or insured credit.

Do not expect open account terms on a first order.

### 6. Escrow and secure payment services

Some services hold your money until you confirm that the goods match the agreement. This can reduce risk on smaller deals, but rules, fees and dispute processes vary. Read the terms carefully, and check how disputes are decided and how long funds are held. Ask your bank or a trade adviser what is available for your route.

## How to protect yourself, whichever method you use

1. **Verify the supplier first.** Check their IEC, GST and company records. See our guide on [verifying an Indian supplier]({{ '/2026/11/22/how-to-verify-indian-supplier-iec-gst-mca-checks/' | relative_url }}).
2. **Pay only into an account in the supplier's legal name.** A request to pay a different person or company is a major red flag.
3. **Confirm bank details by phone or video call** using contact details you found independently, not only those in the email. Payment-redirection fraud, where a fraudster impersonates a supplier by email and changes the bank details, is common in international trade.
4. **Tie payment to milestones.** Pay against production proof, inspection or shipping documents, not only on promises.
5. **Order an independent inspection** before paying the balance, especially for larger orders.
6. **Put it in writing.** The contract should state the amount, currency, due dates, method, bank charges and what happens if goods fail inspection.
7. **Match payment terms to your Incoterm.** Who pays freight and insurance affects the price you pay. See our guide to [Incoterms]({{ '/2026/11/25/incoterms-explained-importing-from-india-exw-fob-cif-ddp/' | relative_url }}).
8. **Check the documents.** Know what you should receive before you release payment. See our guide on [export documents from India]({{ '/2026/11/28/export-documents-from-india-what-buyers-should-receive/' | relative_url }}).

## Currency and bank charges

- **Agree the currency** in the contract. Exchange-rate movement between order and payment can change the real price.
- **Ask who pays bank charges.** Intermediary banks can deduct fees. Choose "our" or "shared" charges according to your agreement, and tell your bank clearly.
- **Keep records** of every payment, including the bank reference.
- Foreign exchange rules apply to exporters in India and to buyers in your country. Ask your bank or a trade adviser about requirements on both sides.

## Warning signs

- Pressure to pay quickly, or a big discount for full advance payment
- A bank account in a different name from the supplier's legal name
- Last-minute change of bank details sent only by email
- Payment requested through a personal account or an unusual channel
- A supplier who refuses any form of protection, such as inspection or a split payment
- A price far below every other quote

## Payment safety checklist

- ✅ Supplier verified through official records
- ✅ Payment method and milestones written in the contract
- ✅ Bank account name matches the supplier's verified legal name
- ✅ Bank details confirmed through an independent contact
- ✅ Deposit kept small, balance tied to inspection or documents
- ✅ Independent inspection arranged for larger orders
- ✅ Currency, bank charges and due dates agreed in writing
- ✅ Incoterm matches who pays for freight and insurance
- ✅ Records of every payment and message saved

## Common mistakes

- **Sending the full amount on the first order** to a supplier you found online.
- **Trusting a changed bank detail** without calling to confirm.
- **Assuming an LC guarantees quality.** It guarantees payment against documents.
- **Ignoring bank charges.** They can add up on small orders.
- **Leaving payment terms vague**, which leads to disputes when something goes wrong.

## FAQ

**What is the safest way to pay an Indian supplier?**
There is no single safest method. For larger orders, a letter of credit combined with an independent inspection gives strong protection to both sides. For smaller first orders, a split payment tied to inspection is common.

**How much deposit is normal?**
It varies by product and supplier. Agree a figure that you are comfortable losing in the worst case, and tie the balance to proof of shipment or inspection.

**Can I use a credit card or an online payment service?**
For small orders and samples, some suppliers accept them. Check fees and buyer-protection rules, which differ by provider.

**Is a letter of credit worth the cost?**
For high-value orders or new relationships, many buyers think so. For small orders, the fees and paperwork may outweigh the benefit.

**What should I do if I think I have paid the wrong account?**
Contact your bank immediately and ask if the payment can be recalled. Speed matters.

## Quick answer

Trade finance answers two questions: who carries the payment risk, and when does each side get paid? At one end, advance payment favours the seller; at the other, an open account favours the buyer. A letter of credit sits in the middle by substituting a bank's undertaking for the buyer's promise, but it only works if the documents match the credit exactly. Choose the instrument based on trust, leverage, and jurisdiction — then make the paperwork flawless.

*This is an educational overview, not banking, legal, or foreign-exchange advice. Instrument availability, documentation rules, and exchange controls vary by country and change over time; confirm details with your bank and official sources.*

## 1. Overview of Cross-Border Trade Finance

International commercial transactions involve inherent risks due to geographic distance, unfamiliar legal jurisdictions, and credit exposure between buyers and sellers. Trade finance instruments bridge this gap by providing liquidity, securing payment guarantees, and regulating the transfer of cargo title documents.

The core tension is simple. A seller wants cash before parting with goods; a buyer wants goods before releasing cash. Each settlement method moves that risk somewhere different. Trade finance also solves a practical problem: banks and insurers are willing to act as intermediaries because they can examine documents rather than assess the commercial quality of the goods themselves. That is why paperwork precision matters so much in this field.

## 2. Core Trade Settlement Instruments

International trade relies on several standardized payment frameworks structured around varying risk tolerances:

| Settlement Instrument | Risk Distribution | Operational Workflow |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **1. Advance Payment** | High Risk for Buyer / Low Risk for Seller | Buyer transfers funds prior to shipment dispatch. Used typically for custom or high-demand goods. |
| **2. Letter of Credit (LC)** | Balanced (Bank-Backed Guarantee) | Opening bank guarantees payment to the beneficiary upon presentation of strict compliant shipping documents. |
| **3. Documentary Collection** | Balanced (Bank-Mediated Collection) | Exporter's bank forwards shipping documents to importer's bank against payment or acceptance of a draft. |
| **4. Open Account** | High Risk for Seller / Low Risk for Buyer | Goods and documents are shipped directly; buyer remits payment on agreed net terms (e.g., Net 60). |

---

### How to read the risk column

No method removes risk; it assigns it. Advance payment gives the seller certainty but exposes the buyer to non-delivery. Open account does the reverse. An LC reduces counterparty risk but introduces documentary risk — a typo can delay payment. Documentary collection is usually cheaper than an LC, yet banks act as messengers rather than guarantors, so the seller still carries credit risk.

Some transactions combine methods: a deposit up front, the balance against documents, and a retention amount after acceptance. Milestone structures are common for custom-manufactured goods, where the seller needs funding before committing materials.

### Practical selection checklist

| Question | If yes | Likely direction |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Is this a first transaction with an unknown counterparty? | Yes | Advance payment or LC |
| Is the buyer a long-standing, creditworthy partner? | Yes | Open account may be reasonable |
| Are you making bespoke goods the buyer cannot resell easily? | Yes | Seek a deposit or milestone payments |
| Is the buyer's country subject to exchange controls? | Yes | Confirm an LC or bank route is workable before quoting |
| Are documents complex or regulated? | Yes | Allow more time and use specialist review |
| Is the order small and repeatable? | Yes | Simple terms may be more cost-effective |

## 3. Letter of Credit (LC) Workflow Mechanics

A Letter of Credit minimizes counterparty risk by replacing individual corporate credit with institutional bank backing. The lifecycle follows four key stages:

### Issuance and Advising
The buyer (applicant) requests their bank (issuing bank) to open an LC in favor of the seller (beneficiary). The issuing bank transmits this via an advising bank in the seller's country.

### Document Presentation
Upon dispatching the cargo, the seller submits required documentation—such as the Bill of Lading, Commercial Invoice, and Certificate of Origin—to the negotiating bank.

### Verification and Settlement
The banks examine all paperwork strictly against the LC terms. If documents conform, the issuing bank honors the payment or accepts a deferred bill of exchange.

### What "strict compliance" means in practice

Banks deal in documents, not goods. If the credit calls for a specific notation and the document says something else, the discrepancy must be resolved — usually by obtaining a waiver from the applicant or correcting the document within the allowed presentation period. Common friction points include inconsistent dates, a description that differs from the LC wording, missing signatures, or a late presentation.

Before goods are shipped, the seller should review the LC line by line. If a requirement is impossible or unreasonable, request an amendment rather than hoping a bank will overlook it. Amendments take time and require agreement from the relevant parties.

### Typical document set to reconcile

| Document | Check against |
| :--- | :--- |
| Commercial invoice | LC description, currency, amount, and buyer details |
| Transport document | Shipment date, ports, consignee, and required notations |
| Certificate of origin | Issuer, origin statement, and required signatures |
| Insurance document | Cover amount and date relative to shipment, if required |
| Inspection or packing certificate | Required wording and authorized signatory |
| Draft or bill of exchange | Tenor and addressee specified in the credit |

## 4. Risk and compliance notes

* **Documentary risk is real.** A compliant presentation is what triggers payment under an LC; accuracy matters more than speed.
* **Currency exposure:** If invoice and funding currencies differ, agree who bears exchange-rate movement. Banks can explain available hedging tools.
* **Sanctions and restricted parties:** Screen all parties and carriers where applicable. Rules change frequently — verify with official sources.
* **Fraud indicators:** Unusual payment instructions, last-minute bank-detail changes, or pressure to bypass the agreed route warrant a direct verification call using a known contact.
* **Recordkeeping:** Keep the credit, amendments, presentations, and bank messages together for audit and dispute handling.
* **Jurisdiction:** Confirm which country's law governs the instrument and where disputes would be heard.

## 5. Common mistakes

| Mistake | Consequence | Simple fix |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Accepting an LC without reading it | Impossible conditions and delayed payment | Review every clause before production starts |
| Invoice wording copied from a quotation | Discrepancy against the LC description | Match the credit's exact goods description |
| Presenting documents after the expiry date | Bank refuses payment | Track presentation windows and ship early |
| Assuming advance payment removes all risk | Buyer loses leverage if goods never arrive | Use milestones, inspection rights, or escrow where available |
| Changing bank details over email alone | Payment diverted by fraud | Verify any change through a separately established channel |

## 6. Frequently asked questions

**Is a letter of credit a guarantee of payment?** It is a bank undertaking to pay against compliant documents, not a guarantee that the goods are satisfactory. Inspect goods independently.

**Which method is cheapest?** Open account usually has the lowest bank cost but the highest credit risk for the seller. Compare cost against risk, not in isolation.

**What happens if documents have a discrepancy?** The bank may refuse or seek a waiver from the applicant. Resolution depends on the buyer and the nature of the discrepancy.

**Can an LC be cancelled?** Generally only with the agreement of the parties involved, since it is a bank undertaking. Check the credit's terms and ask your bank.

**How does an Incoterms rule affect payment?** It defines delivery, cost, and risk transfer — not payment method. Confirm both in the contract. See our [Incoterms guide]({{ '/2026/11/25/incoterms-explained-importing-from-india-exw-fob-cif-ddp/' | relative_url }}).

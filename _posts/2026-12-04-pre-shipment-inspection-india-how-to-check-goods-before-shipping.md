A sample that looks perfect does not guarantee that the 5,000 units in your order will match it. Production runs vary, shortcuts happen and packing mistakes are common. Once the container leaves India, fixing a problem becomes slow and expensive.

An inspection before shipment is the cheapest way to find out what is really in the boxes while you can still do something about it.

## Quick answer

For any order of meaningful value, arrange an **independent inspection** before you pay the balance, usually when production is complete and most goods are packed. Give the inspector a **written brief** with your specification, approved sample, test requirements and the standard for acceptable defects. Tie your final payment to a **pass result**, or to a corrective action plan if it fails.

## Types of inspection

| Type | When it happens | What it checks | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pre-production check | Before production starts | Raw materials, components, supplier readiness | New suppliers and custom products |
| During production check | When 20 to 60 percent of the order is made (timing varies) | Early defects, process control | Large or long production runs |
| Pre-shipment inspection | When production is complete and mostly packed | Quantity, quality, packaging, marking, sampled testing | Almost every order |
| Container loading check | During loading of the container | Right goods, right quantity, safe loading and sealing | High-value or high-risk shipments |
| Factory audit | Before choosing a supplier | Capacity, management, working conditions, quality systems | New suppliers and long-term partners |

Most buyers start with a pre-shipment inspection. Add the others as order value and risk go up.

## Who can inspect

- **Independent inspection companies** with local inspectors. They are used widely and give you a neutral report.
- **Your own staff or agent** in India. This can work well if they know the product.
- **The supplier's own quality team.** Useful, but not independent, so it should not be your only check.

Choose an inspector who is independent of the supplier. Check how they report, what they inspect and how they handle disputes before booking.

## Understand AQL sampling

You do not open every carton. Inspectors draw a random sample and count defects against an agreed limit. The common system is **AQL** (Acceptable Quality Limit), based on international sampling standards.

In simple terms:

1. The inspector selects a sample size based on the **total order quantity** and the **inspection level**.
2. They examine the sample and classify defects as **critical**, **major** or **minor**.
3. The batch **passes** if defects stay within the limits for each class, and **fails** if they exceed them.

| Defect class | Meaning | Common approach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Critical | Unsafe or illegal, or breaks a mandatory regulation | Zero tolerated |
| Major | Makes the product unusable or likely to be returned | A low AQL limit, for example 2.5 (commonly used for consumer goods) |
| Minor | Small cosmetic or finishing issue | A higher limit, for example 4.0 |

The AQL numbers above are common starting points, not rules. Set limits that fit your product, price and customer. Safety-related, medical or regulated products need stricter limits.

## What to put in your inspection brief

The inspector can only check what you describe. A good brief includes:

- **Product specification:** dimensions, materials, colours, finishes and tolerances
- **Approved sample:** a signed "golden sample" that the inspector and the supplier both hold
- **Defect list:** what counts as critical, major and minor for your product
- **AQL level and limits**
- **Functional and safety tests:** for example, load tests, electrical checks or fit tests
- **Packaging and labelling requirements:** carton marks, barcodes, language, country of origin
- **Quantity and packing checks:** units per carton, carton count, weights
- **Photos and measurements to include in the report**
- **Product-specific certificates** you expect to see

If the product is covered by rules in your country, such as labelling, safety or chemical limits, add those requirements. Ask your importer or customs broker if you are unsure.

## What the inspector typically checks

- Quantity ready and packed, against your order
- Workmanship and appearance
- Dimensions and weight against specification
- Colour and finish against the approved sample
- Function tests, for products that work or move
- Labels, markings and barcodes
- Packaging strength and carton condition
- Random carton drop or shipping tests, if requested
- The state of the unfinished goods, so you know if the order is truly ready

## Read the report properly

A useful report gives you:

- **A clear result:** pass, fail or pending, with the defect count against the limits
- **Photos** of defects, packaging and marks
- **Measurements** with actual numbers, not only "OK"
- **Notes** on anything unusual, such as unfinished goods, different materials or rushed packing

Be cautious about reports that show only a tick-box and a few generic photos.

## What to do with the result

| Result | What you can do |
| --- | --- |
| Pass | Release the agreed balance payment and approve shipping |
| Pass with comments | Ask the supplier to fix minor issues before shipping |
| Fail | Request rework, sorting or replacement, then re-inspect before paying the balance |
| Pending | Production not complete or access refused, so reschedule and ask for the reason |

Agree these consequences in your contract before production. If a failed inspection has no consequence, the supplier has little reason to take it seriously. ## Cost and timing

Inspection fees depend on the product, factory location, number of inspector days and travel. Ask for a written quote before booking. For a first order or a large shipment, the fee is small compared with the cost of receiving unsellable goods.

Book the inspector early. Good inspectors have limited availability, and you need time to react if the goods fail.

## When an inspection is especially worth it

- First order with a new supplier
- Custom or branded products
- High-value shipments
- Products with safety or regulatory requirements
- A supplier who has had quality problems before
- Goods you cannot easily return or resell

## Pre-shipment inspection checklist

- ✅ Supplier verified through official records
- ✅ Product specification and approved golden sample agreed in writing
- ✅ Defect definitions and AQL limits set
- ✅ Independent inspector booked with a written quote
- ✅ Inspection brief shared with the inspector and the supplier
- ✅ Inspection date set when production is complete and goods are packed
- ✅ Report received with photos and measurements
- ✅ Payment of the balance tied to a pass or an agreed fix
- ✅ Re-inspection agreed for a failed result
- ✅ Packing list and labels checked against the contract

## Common mistakes

- **Inspecting too early.** If production is not finished, the result tells you little.
- **No approved sample.** Without one, "good quality" is a matter of opinion.
- **Using the supplier's own report as the only check.**
- **Vague defect rules.** The inspector and the supplier will argue about what counts as a defect.
- **Paying the balance before the result.** You lose your leverage.
- **Skipping packaging checks.** Many losses come from poor packing and wrong labels.

## FAQ

**Is an inspection needed for every order?**
Not always. For small repeat orders from a trusted supplier, you might rely on samples and photos. For first orders and larger values, inspection is wise.

**What does AQL 2.5 mean?**
It is a sampling limit for major defects. It sets how many defects in the sample are allowed before the batch fails. The exact numbers depend on the order size and the inspection level.

**Can the supplier arrange the inspection?**
It is better if you appoint the inspector, so the result is independent.

**What if the goods fail?**
Ask for rework or replacement, agree a deadline and re-inspect before you pay the balance or release the shipment.

**Does an inspection replace testing for regulated products?**
No. Safety, chemical or food rules may need lab tests and certificates. Check your import requirements and ask your customs broker.

**Where does this fit in the import process?**
After you verify the supplier and agree payment terms, before shipment.

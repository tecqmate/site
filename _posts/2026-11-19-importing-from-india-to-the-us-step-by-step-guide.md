India is a major source of textiles, home décor, spices, handicrafts, chemicals, incense, and many other goods for US buyers. Importing is very doable for a small business, but it has more steps than a normal purchase. This guide walks through them in order.

*Customs rules and tariffs change often, and they depend on your product. This is general information, not legal or customs advice. Confirm details with a licensed customs broker.*

## Step 1: Identify your product's HTS code

Every product imported into the US is classified under the **Harmonized Tariff Schedule (HTS)**. The code (up to 10 digits) decides your base duty rate and which agencies may regulate the product.

* Ask your supplier for the HS code they use. The first six digits are international, and the US adds more digits.
* Search the official HTS database to find the exact US code and rate.
* If you are unsure, ask your customs broker to confirm the classification. A wrong code can mean the wrong duty and delays.

## Step 2: Check whether your product has special requirements

Some goods need approval or inspection by another US agency, for example:

* Food, cosmetics, and medical products: the FDA.
* Consumer products, toys, and children's items: product safety rules.
* Plants, wood, and agricultural products: agricultural inspection rules.

Also check **country-of-origin marking**: most imported goods must be marked with their origin, such as "Made in India". Do this before you place a large order.

## Step 3: Set up as an importer

* **Importer of record:** the person or company responsible for the shipment and the duties. You will need a business entity and a tax ID (EIN), and you register with US Customs and Border Protection (CBP).
* **Customs broker:** a licensed professional who files your entry with CBP. For most small importers this is the safest way to start.
* **Customs bond:** shipments with a value above about $2,500 generally need a bond. You can buy a bond for a single shipment or a continuous bond for ongoing imports.

## Step 4: Find and verify your supplier

* Ask for the supplier's business registration, **IEC** (Importer-Exporter Code), GST details, and a verifiable address.
* Request samples of the exact product and test them.
* Check references and past export experience to the US.
* Agree product specs, packaging, and labeling in writing.

For a worked example of supplier checks, see our guide to [sourcing incense sticks from India]({{ '/2026/11/17/how-to-source-incense-sticks-from-india-buyers-guide/' | relative_url }}).

## Step 5: Agree on price, Incoterms, and payment

* **Incoterms** define who pays for and manages each part of the journey. Common ones are FOB (you arrange freight from the Indian port), CIF (the supplier pays freight and insurance to your port), and EXW (you collect from the factory).
* Compare quotes on the **same Incoterm**.
* For a first order, consider milestone payments or a letter of credit rather than paying everything upfront.

## Step 6: Arrange shipping

* **Ocean freight** is cheaper but takes weeks. **Air freight** is faster and costs more.
* A **freight forwarder** can book space, handle documents, and coordinate with your broker.
* For ocean shipments, an **Importer Security Filing (ISF)** must be submitted before the cargo is loaded at the foreign port. Your broker usually files it, so give them details early.
* Insure the cargo.

## Step 7: Understand duties, tariffs, and fees

Your total import cost is usually:

> **Goods + international freight and insurance + duty and tariffs + customs fees + broker and bond fees + delivery to your warehouse**

* **Duty** is based on the HTS rate and the customs value of the goods. US customs value is generally the price you paid, **not including international freight and insurance**.
* **Additional tariffs** may apply on top of the base duty, and these have changed several times in 2025 and 2026. As of early November 2026, trade-law commentary reported that no India-US trade deal had been signed, and that the 18% rate announced in a February 2026 framework was never applied. Check the current rate for your exact product and origin before you price your goods.
* **Customs fees** such as the merchandise processing fee, and the harbor maintenance fee for ocean shipments, are added.

**Illustrative example only, not a quote:** goods $10,000 (FOB), freight and insurance $1,300, duty and tariffs at an example 5% of $10,000 = $500, broker, bond, and fees $400. Estimated landed cost before delivery: about $12,200. Your real numbers depend on your product and the current tariff rules.

## Step 8: Customs clearance and delivery

* Your broker files the entry, and you pay duties and fees.
* CBP may inspect or ask for documents, so keep your invoice, packing list, and bill of lading ready.
* Once cleared, the carrier delivers to your location.
* **Keep records** of the shipment for at least five years.

## Common mistakes

1. **Guessing the HTS code** instead of confirming it.
2. **Ignoring other agency rules** (food, cosmetics, safety, labeling).
3. **Comparing prices on different Incoterms.**
4. **Not budgeting** for tariffs, customs fees, broker, and delivery.
5. **Paying 100% advance** to an unverified supplier.
6. **Late ISF filing** for ocean shipments.
7. **Assuming tariffs will stay the same** between the time you order and the time goods arrive.

## Quick checklist

* HTS code confirmed and duty rate checked.
* Product rules and marking requirements checked.
* Importer of record, broker, and bond arranged.
* Supplier verified and samples approved.
* Incoterm, price, and payment terms agreed in writing.
* Freight, insurance, and ISF planned.
* Landed-cost estimate prepared with a margin for tariff changes.
* Records stored for five years.

## FAQ

**Do I need a license to import from India?**
There is no general US import license for most goods, but you need to meet registration, bond, and product-specific requirements.

**Can I import without a customs broker?**
You can file yourself, but most small importers use a licensed broker because the process and software are complex.

**How long does shipping take?**
Ocean shipments usually take weeks and air shipments days, plus customs clearance and delivery time. Ask your forwarder for current estimates.

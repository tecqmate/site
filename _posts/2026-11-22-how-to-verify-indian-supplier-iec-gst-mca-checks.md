Most problems with overseas suppliers can be spotted before any money moves. For India, three free government records do most of the work: the Import Export Code (IEC), the GST registration and, for companies, the Ministry of Corporate Affairs (MCA) record.

This guide shows what each check tells you, how to run it, and where registration checks stop being useful.

## Quick answer

Confirm that the **legal name matches across the IEC, GST and MCA records**, that each registration is **active**, and that the address and business activity make sense for the product. Then go beyond paperwork: samples, a factory check for larger orders, and safe payment terms. A name mismatch between records is a reason to pause.

## Before you start: ask for the right details

Ask the supplier, in writing, for:

- Legal (registered) name and trade name
- IEC number
- GSTIN
- CIN, if it is a company (or LLPIN for an LLP)
- Registered address and factory or warehouse address
- Product-specific licences or certificates you need

A genuine supplier will share these without fuss. Hesitation is information.

## Check 1: the Import Export Code (IEC)

**What it shows:** that the firm is registered to export from India, along with its legal name, address and status.

**How:** use the official DGFT website. Look for the option to view or verify an IEC, and search by the IEC number or firm name. You can also check on the customs portal, ICEGATE.

**What to look for:**
- The legal name matches what the supplier uses on invoices
- The status is active, not suspended or cancelled
- The address is consistent with the other records

A trader selling exports without an IEC is a deal-breaker for most buyers.

## Check 2: the GST registration

**What it shows:** the legal and trade name, registered address, and whether the taxpayer is active, suspended or cancelled.

**How:** use the "Search Taxpayer" function on the official GST portal and enter the GSTIN.

**What to look for:**
- Legal name consistent with the IEC
- Status is active
- The registered business type fits what they claim to be (manufacturer, trader, service provider)

A cancelled or suspended GST registration is a serious red flag.

## Check 3: the MCA record (for companies and LLPs)

**What it shows:** whether a company legally exists and is in good standing, its CIN, incorporation date, registered address and directors.

**How:** on the MCA portal, use the option to view company or LLP data, and search by name or CIN.

**What to look for:**
- The company name and CIN match what the supplier gave you
- The company is active, not struck off
- The incorporation date fits their story. "20 years of experience" and a company registered last year need an explanation.

Proprietorships and partnerships do not appear in MCA company records, so for these, GST and IEC matter more.

## Cross-check: do the three records agree?

| Check | Record | Should match |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Name | IEC, GST, MCA | The same legal entity |
| Address | IEC, GST, MCA | Same city and broadly the same address |
| Status | IEC, GST, MCA | Active on all three |
| Business type | GST, MCA | Fits the product they sell |
| Bank account | Invoice or proforma | In the same legal name |

If the account name on the proforma invoice differs from the legal name, stop and ask why.

## What registration checks do not tell you

Portals confirm that a business is registered. They do not confirm quality, capacity or honesty. Combine them with:

1. **Samples.** Test several production batches, not only a hand-picked sample.
2. **Factory or warehouse verification.** For larger orders, get a visit or an independent inspection. The factory address often differs from the registered one.
3. **Certificates.** Fake certificates exist. Verify them on the issuing body's own site.
4. **References.** Ask for previous export customers in your region and contact them.
5. **Merchant exporters.** If the seller is a trader, ask who actually makes the product and whether you can see that site.

## Red flags

- Legal names that differ across IEC, GST and MCA
- Registration that is new, or suspended or cancelled
- Pressure to pay quickly, or a discount for full advance payment
- A payment account in an individual's or different company's name
- Only a messaging app contact, no business email or address
- Photos that look like stock images or appear under another company's name
- Prices far below every other quote you have received
- Refusal to provide a sample or a written specification

## Payment safety

- Avoid paying 100% in advance to a new supplier.
- Use a staged structure, such as a deposit against a proforma invoice and the balance against shipping documents.
- Match the bank account name to the verified legal name every time.
- Keep a written purchase agreement with specifications, tolerances, delivery date and what happens if goods fail inspection.
- For larger orders, consider a secure payment instrument offered through your bank. Ask your bank what is available for your route.

## A 15-minute verification checklist

- [ ] Collected legal name, IEC, GSTIN and CIN in writing
- [ ] IEC verified and active
- [ ] GST verified and active
- [ ] MCA record verified (if a company)
- [ ] Names and addresses match across records
- [ ] Bank account name matches the legal name
- [ ] Product-specific certificates verified at the source
- [ ] Sample tested, and a retained sample kept
- [ ] Factory or warehouse check planned for larger orders
- [ ] Payment terms agreed in writing

## FAQ

**Can I verify an Indian supplier entirely online?**
Partly. IEC, GST and MCA records can be checked online, but capacity, quality and working conditions need samples, inspections or independent verification.

**Is a GST number proof that a supplier is reliable?**
No. It shows they are registered for tax and whether the registration is active. It says nothing about product quality or delivery.

**What if the supplier is a trader, not a manufacturer?**
That is not a problem by itself. Verify the trader with the same checks, and ask which manufacturer produces your goods.

**Should I pay for a third-party inspection?**
For a first order of meaningful value, usually yes. The cost is small compared with a rejected shipment.

If you ask three agencies to quote for "an app like Uber but for plumbers", you will get three very different numbers, and none of them will be reliable. The quotes differ because each team is guessing a different project.

A clear brief fixes this. It does not need to be long or technical. It needs to answer the questions a good team would ask on a first call, in writing, so every vendor prices the same thing.

## Quick answer

A good software brief covers ten things: the business goal, users, core features, what is out of scope, existing systems, technical constraints, design expectations, timeline, budget range and how you will judge proposals. One to three pages is enough.

## Why a brief matters

- **Comparable quotes.** When every vendor reads the same document, you can compare like with like.
- **Fewer surprises.** Most budget overruns start with unwritten assumptions.
- **Better vendors respond.** Experienced teams are more willing to bid on a project that is clearly defined.
- **A base for the contract.** The brief can feed directly into scope and acceptance criteria.

## The 10 sections of a strong brief

### 1. Company and project summary

Two or three sentences: who you are, what the product is and why you are building it now.

*Example:* "We are a UK-based logistics company. We want a web portal where customers can book and track deliveries, replacing our email and spreadsheet process."

### 2. Business goal and success measures

State the outcome, not only the output. What should change when the project works?

- Reduce manual booking time
- Increase online orders
- Cut support emails

Where you can, add a simple measure, such as "booking takes under two minutes". Do not invent numbers you cannot track.

### 3. Users and their main tasks

List each type of user (customer, admin, driver) and the three to five things each must be able to do. This is the single most useful part for a development team.

### 4. Core features (must-have, should-have, later)

Group features by priority rather than writing one long list.

| Priority | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Must-have | Launch is not possible without it | User login, booking form |
| Should-have | Important, but launch can survive without it | Email notifications |
| Later | Good idea for a future version | Mobile app |
{: .table .table-sm .table-bordered .table-striped .align-top }

A clear "must-have" list is the main protection against scope growth.

### 5. Out of scope

Say what is not included: for example, native mobile apps, data migration from an old system, or content writing. Writing this down avoids arguments later.

### 6. Existing systems and integrations

List any tools the new product must work with: payment gateway, CRM, accounting software, ERP, email service, identity provider. Mention who owns each account and whether API documentation exists.

### 7. Technical and compliance constraints

Include anything that limits the choices:

- Preferred technologies, or "open to recommendations"
- Hosting location or data residency needs
- Privacy and data protection rules that apply to your users (for example GDPR or UK GDPR)
- Accessibility expectations
- Security requirements, such as audit trails or role-based access

If you are not sure what applies to you, say so and ask vendors to explain what they would recommend.

### 8. Design expectations

Do you have brand guidelines, wireframes or examples you like? Share two or three reference sites and say what you like about each. If you have no design at all, say that too, so vendors include design in the quote.

### 9. Timeline and budget range

Give a target launch date and the reason for it (an event, a contract, a season). Also give a budget range, even a wide one.

Many buyers hide the budget in the hope of lower quotes. This usually backfires: vendors either overshoot or propose the wrong solution. A range lets teams suggest a realistic scope, or a phased plan that fits.

### 10. How you will evaluate proposals

Tell vendors what matters and what you need in the response:

- Understanding of your problem
- Relevant past work and references
- Proposed team and who will do the work
- Approach and milestones
- Pricing model and what is included
- Support after launch
- Ownership of code and data

## Questions to ask every vendor to answer

Add a short section at the end of the brief asking vendors to respond to these:

1. Who will work on my project, and what is their role?
2. What assumptions have you made in your estimate?
3. What would you change or cut to reduce cost?
4. How do you handle changes after the work starts?
5. Who owns the source code and the accounts once the project is complete?
6. What happens after launch: warranty, bug fixes, support?
7. How will we communicate, and which time zones will overlap?

Comparing the answers to these questions is often more useful than comparing the prices.

## A simple template you can copy

```
PROJECT BRIEF

1. About us and the project (2-3 sentences)
2. Goal and how we will measure success
3. Users and their main tasks
4. Features: must-have / should-have / later
5. Out of scope
6. Existing systems and integrations
7. Technical, security and compliance constraints
8. Design references and brand assets
9. Target launch date and budget range
10. How we will evaluate proposals, and what we need in your reply
```

## Common mistakes

- **Describing the solution instead of the problem.** Say what users need to do, and let the team propose how.
- **Skipping the budget.** It leads to guesswork on both sides.
- **No "out of scope" list.** The boundary is exactly where most disputes begin.
- **Sending the brief to too many vendors.** Three to five well-chosen teams are easier to evaluate than fifteen.
- **Treating the brief as final.** Expect good vendors to ask questions and suggest improvements. That is a good sign.

## What to do after you send the brief

1. Allow time for questions, and share every answer with all vendors.
2. Ask for a short call with each shortlisted team.
3. Request a breakdown by phase, not only one total.

## FAQ

**How long should a project brief be?**
Usually one to three pages. Clear and specific is better than long.

**Do I need a technical person to write one?**
No. Focus on users, goals and outcomes. A good vendor will help with the technical side.

**Should I include my budget?**
A range is recommended. It helps vendors propose something realistic instead of guessing.

**What is the difference between a brief and an RFP?**
A brief describes the project. An RFP (request for proposal) is a brief plus rules for how vendors should respond and how you will choose. For small projects, one document can do both.

**Can I change the brief after sending it?**
Yes, but send the update to every vendor so the quotes stay comparable.
